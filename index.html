Chapter 1

A decade of collisions

Collisions get reported by the thousands every year — a mix of city growth, more vehicles on the road, and how consistently incidents get reported. Before asking who's most at risk, it's worth seeing the shape of the problem over time.

The bars below split each year's collisions by outcome: injury vs. property-damage only. Fatal collisions are covered separately in the next chapter — they're rare enough (under 1% of all collisions) that stacking them in here would make them invisible, and they deserve a more careful comparison than "which bar is biggest" anyway.

With this we can track whether collision volume is rising faster or slower than the city's population/traffic growth.

Chapter 2

Fatality risk is not equal

Fatal collisions are a small fraction of the total. However, what matters more is the fatality rate: out of everyone involved in a collision as a given road-user type, what share died?

Automobiles have steel and airbags around them; pedestrians and cyclists have nothing. That structural difference is the likely reason the rate tends to run higher for the road users with the least physical protection, even though they're a minority of all collisions.

This is why we should prioritize protection, not just volume of collisions, when allocating safety budget.

Chapter 3

Frequent doesn't mean dangerous

Automobile-only collisions are by far the most common — mechanically, that's because automobiles are the overwhelming majority of vehicles on the road, not because driving is uniquely dangerous. Look instead at the share of each road-user group's collisions that end in injury rather than just property damage.

Toggle to see raw counts or the share within each group.

This way we compare outcome severity per road-user type without volume distorting the picture — the % view is the fairer basis for arguing where protective infrastructure matters most.

Chapter 4

Risk varies by division

Zooming from road-user type to geography: some police divisions record far more severe outcomes than others, even after accounting for volume. Likely reasons include busier arterial roads, more nightlife/foot traffic, or larger geographic coverage — not necessarily "more dangerous drivers." This narrows "citywide problem" down to specific areas worth investigating first.

Top divisions by total collision count, split by outcome.

Useful for prioritizing where to direct engineering review or enforcement budget, though it should be weighed against each division's population and road-length before concluding it's relatively more dangerous, not just busier.

Reading the axis: codes like D42 or D32 are Toronto Police operational divisions — geographic patrol areas, not neighbourhood names. Chapter 7

What actually fixes this

Every chapter so far pointed at a cause. Here's the countermeasure that matches each one — click a fix to see where the data suggests it would help most.

Aggressive / distracted driving

1. Rumble strips

Creates vibration and sound to alert distracted or drowsy drivers drifting out of their lane.

At intersections

2. Intersection safety

Redesigning intersections with better signals, clear markings, and protected phases for pedestrians reduces conflict points.

Night time

3. Street lighting

Increases visibility at intersections and along corridors — critical for spotting pedestrians, who are disproportionately struck after dark.

Uncontrolled crossings

4. Stop signs & signals

Adding or upgrading traffic control at uncontrolled crossings gives pedestrians a protected right-of-way.

Locations are the top-ranked neighbourhoods by the closest available proxy in this dataset for each cause (e.g. "night time" uses pedestrian collisions during night hours) — a starting shortlist for engineering review, not a substitute for a site survey.

Where this leaves us

The case for acting on specific hours and places

Across five years of records, the pattern holds: risk is not evenly spread. It concentrates in particular hours, falls hardest on pedestrians and cyclists relative to their numbers, and clusters in identifiable divisions and neighbourhoods. That's exactly where targeted street redesign, lighting, and enforcement would do the most good per dollar spent.
